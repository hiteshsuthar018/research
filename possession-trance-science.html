Devta aana, examined

    A god arrives on Saturday. What is happening?

    Every week in a village, one man's face, posture and voice change. He strikes his own back with an iron chain and seems not to suffer. People ask him about illness, money, marriage. He answers, and hands them a few grains.

    This page does not ask “real or fake?” It asks a better question: which parts of this can be measured, and what do the measurements actually show?

    Supported Repeatedly observed in published studies.
    Proposed A researcher's hypothesis; evidence thin or mixed.
    Outside science Not a question measurement can settle.
    01 / the case

    Start with the man, not with a verdict

    A village in India. Every Saturday, people gather for the local deity. Drums, a crowd, a familiar sequence of acts. One particular man begins to shake. His expression hardens, his movements change, sometimes his voice drops or rises into a register nobody hears from him on an ordinary day. The community says: devta aa gaye — the deity has come.

    He takes an iron chain and strikes his own back, hard, repeatedly. He does not flinch the way you would expect. Then people come forward. A mother asks about her son's fever. A man asks whether to sell his land. A family asks why a marriage keeps falling through. He answers, sometimes sharply, sometimes in short riddles. He presses a handful of grain into their palms and tells them what to do with it.

    An hour later, the state passes. He may say he remembers little. On Monday he is a farmer, a shopkeeper, a neighbour.

    There are two lazy responses to this scene, and both of them stop the enquiry before it begins.

    “He is obviously acting.”

    This assumes the answer. Deliberate faking is one real possibility among several, and it does happen — but a person can also enter a state they did not choose, with changes in attention, memory and bodily control that they cannot simply switch on at will. Assuming performance means you never look for the mechanism.

    “A deity is clearly inside him.”

    This also assumes the answer. It may be what he and his community sincerely believe, and this page will not mock that. But it is a claim about an unmeasured entity, and everything observable in the scene — the trembling, the changed voice, the tolerated blows — has candidate explanations that can be studied directly.

    The scientific move is to split the scene into parts that can be observed, parts that have known mechanisms, and parts that no instrument reaches. That is the structure of this entire page, and it is the structure of the answer at the end.

    One thing to fix in your head before reading further

    The states described here are not rare, not exotic, and not a sign that something is wrong with a person. Anthropologists surveying hundreds of societies found culturally patterned altered states of consciousness in the large majority of them — the older landmark survey by Erika Bourguignon reported something like 90% of 488 societies, and although that count used loose criteria by modern standards, later work has not overturned the basic picture. Whatever is happening on that Saturday, the human capacity behind it appears to be close to universal. What changes from place to place is what the state is called, who is allowed to enter it, and what is expected once they do.

    02 / vocabulary

    Twelve words people use for one scene

    Most confusion about possession comes from mixing vocabularies. A word borrowed from psychiatry sounds like a diagnosis; a word borrowed from anthropology sounds like an endorsement. They are describing different slices of the same Saturday.

    Who owns which word

    TermField it comes fromWhat it actually means
    Spirit possessionAnthropology / religionA cultural explanation: a non-human agent is acting through a person's body. It describes a belief system, not a measurement.
    Possession tranceAnthropologyThe observable version: an altered state in which the person's identity is experienced and displayed as replaced by another agent. Neutral about whether the agent exists.
    Religious / cultural tranceAnthropologyTrance that is expected, scheduled, taught and valued by a community. The Saturday ritual is exactly this.
    MediumshipAnthropology / psychology of religionA person who routinely relays messages from another agent. Possession is one form; automatic writing and hearing-voices forms also exist.
    OracleReligious studies / historyA social role: the recognised person a community consults for divine answers. It says nothing about inner experience.
    Altered state of consciousnessPsychology / neuroscienceAny marked, temporary change in how experience is organised — attention, time sense, body awareness, sense of self.
    AbsorptionPsychologyA measurable trait: the tendency to become totally immersed, losing track of surroundings. Tellegen and Atkinson built the standard scale in 1974.
    SuggestibilityPsychologyHow strongly a person's experience shifts in response to expectation and instruction. Varies a lot between individuals and is testable.
    DissociationPsychologyA partial disconnection between things normally joined: action and ownership, experience and memory, body and self. Everyone does it mildly; intensity varies.
    Pathological possessionPsychiatryA possession state that is unwanted, uncontrollable, distressing or disabling — and not sanctioned by the person's own culture.
    Dissociative disordersPsychiatryFormal diagnoses (DSM-5, ICD-11) requiring clinically significant distress or impairment. ICD-11 codes trance disorder as 6B62 and possession trance disorder as 6B63.
    Default mode network, salience networkNeuroscienceNamed patterns of brain regions that tend to activate together. Useful shorthand, not organs, and not “the possession centre”.

    The single most important line in this section

    Both major diagnostic systems explicitly carve out cultural and religious practice. ICD-11's possession trance disorder requires that the state be involuntary and unwanted, that it cause significant distress or impairment, and that it not be accepted as part of a collective cultural or religious practice. On that definition, a village oracle who enters trance on schedule, functions normally on Monday, and holds a respected role is not diagnosable by virtue of the trance itself. A psychiatrist who labelled him ill would be misapplying their own manual.

    03 / the main pathway

    How a Saturday becomes a deity

    This is the backbone of the whole scientific account. Tap any step to open what researchers think is happening there — and how solid that claim is.

    Before anything happens in anyone's brain, a script exists in the community. The deity has a name, a temperament, preferred days, characteristic gestures. Children watch it for years. This is cultural learning, and it supplies the content of everything that follows — not the capacity, but the shape.

    Why it matters mechanically: the brain is not a neutral recorder. It constantly predicts what experience should be like and then fits incoming signals to those predictions. A lifetime of watching possession gives extremely detailed predictions to fit.

    Supported Cross-cultural research consistently finds that the form, content and triggers of possession states track local belief closely.

    Saturday, the same courtyard, the same order of acts. Repetition turns the setting itself into a signal. In learning terms this is conditioning: cues that reliably precede a state start to trigger parts of that state on their own, the way a familiar smell can pull a mood out of you before you have identified it.

    Expectancy is also the active ingredient in hypnosis research: what people are told will happen strongly shapes what they then experience, including experiences they report as involuntary.

    Supported Expectancy effects on experience are among the most replicated findings in psychology. Proposed That ritual cues act as classical conditioned triggers for trance specifically is a reasonable model with limited direct field testing.

    Sustained rhythm is the single most widespread trance-induction technique on earth. In a 2016 fMRI study, experienced shamanic practitioners listening to rhythmic drumming either entered trance or did not, on cue, inside the scanner. During trance, connectivity within the auditory pathway dropped — the brain appeared to be turning down the very drumming that induced the state, which the authors called perceptual decoupling.

    Alongside this: physical exertion, heat, crowd noise, sometimes hunger. These push the body toward high arousal, which matters for pain and for the felt intensity of the experience.

    Supported Rhythmic driving reliably assists trance induction in practised people. Proposed Popular claims that drumming “entrains brainwaves to theta and that causes trance” run far ahead of the evidence. Theta and alpha increases have been recorded — including in a Balinese possession ritual — but a correlation in a handful of people is not a causal mechanism.

    Two things rise together. Arousal is the body's activation: sympathetic nervous system, adrenaline and noradrenaline, faster heart, sharper senses. Attention narrows onto a small set of inputs — the drum, the flame, the deity's image — and drops almost everything else.

    Field studies using portable heart-rate monitors at high-intensity rituals have documented sustained arousal, and in one striking study of Spanish fire-walking, the heart rates of relatives watching the ritual tracked the walkers' heart rates closely — arousal is partly a shared, social event, not just a private one.

    Supported Physiological arousal during intense ritual is measured, repeatedly, in the field.

    Absorption is the point where narrowed attention becomes a changed state. Time distorts. Self-monitoring — the running commentary that checks how you look to others — quiets down. People differ markedly in how easily they get here, and that difference is measurable with standard scales.

    In the drumming fMRI study, trance showed stronger hubs in the posterior cingulate cortex (a core default-mode region tied to internally directed thought) coupled with the dorsal anterior cingulate and insula (salience-network regions that amplify whatever currently counts as important). The authors' reading: an internal stream of experience was being amplified while external input was damped.

    Supported Absorption is a real, measurable individual difference. Proposed The specific network account comes from small samples (n = 15 there) in one tradition, using one induction method. Treat it as a promising lead, not a settled map.

    This is the hinge of the entire phenomenon, and it gets a full section below. In brief: the feeling that you caused your own action is not a direct perception. It is a construction the brain assembles, and it can come apart while the movement itself stays perfectly coordinated.

    When it comes apart inside a ritual where everyone expects a deity, the most available interpretation for that odd feeling is sitting right there: the deity has arrived.

    Supported Sense of agency can be experimentally disrupted while action remains intact — in hypnosis, in illusions, in neurological cases. Proposed That this is the specific mechanism operating in ritual possession is a strong hypothesis, not a demonstrated fact.

    The behaviour is not random. It is recognisably this deity — which tells you it is drawing on learned material. That does not make it fake, any more than speaking grammatical Hindi is fake because you learned the grammar. Deeply practised behaviour can run with very little conscious supervision.

    Supported Possession behaviour reliably follows culturally specific scripts.

    Nobody in the courtyard says “interesting dissociative episode”. They say the deity has come, and they act accordingly — bowing, quieting, forming a queue. The interpretation is applied instantly and unanimously, which makes it very hard for the person in the state to frame their experience any other way.

    Supported How an ambiguous internal state gets labelled strongly shapes how it is subsequently experienced and remembered.

    The state now produces consequences: people listen, defer, ask, thank. Some responses are rewarding, some are simply confirming. Over months and years this shapes which behaviours recur and which fade — the same learning processes that shape any expert social role.

    Careful: this is not the same as saying “he does it for attention”. Reinforcement shapes behaviour without requiring that the person be pursuing the reward, or even aware of it.

    Supported Social reinforcement shapes behaviour. Proposed How much of a given oracle's repertoire is shaped this way, in any specific case, is unknown without study.

    Practised trance gets easier, quicker and more consistent — which is exactly what you would predict of any skill, and exactly what practitioners across traditions report. The loop closes: the more reliably it happens, the stronger the cultural belief, the stronger next week's expectation.

    This is why “he has been doing it for twenty years” is evidence of practice, not evidence of authenticity or of fraud. Both would predict the same improvement.

    Supported Experienced practitioners enter these states faster and more reliably than novices across multiple traditions.

    Read the cascade correctly

    It is a loop, not a line, and no single arrow is “the cause”. Remove the culture and you get a different state with different content. Remove the drumming and it may still happen, more slowly. Remove the community and the role collapses even if the state does not. Causation here is distributed across a whole system — which is precisely why simple verdicts fail.

    04 / the brain

    What might be going on inside the head

    A warning first, because this is where popular explanations go wrong fastest. Nobody has scanned a village oracle mid-possession. Everything below is transferred from adjacent research — hypnosis, absorption, mediumship, dissociation — and transferred knowledge is weaker than direct knowledge. Treat the regions as characters in a plausible story, not as a diagnosis.

    Pick a region

    Each one has a job in ordinary life and a proposed role here. The evidence tag tells you how far to trust the second part.

    Three networks, in plain language

    Default mode network

    Active when you are inward-facing — daydreaming, remembering, narrating yourself to yourself. Includes the posterior cingulate and midline frontal areas. Plausibly relevant because the sense of being a continuous “me” draws on it.

    Salience network

    Insula and dorsal anterior cingulate. It flags what deserves attention right now and hands control to whichever system should deal with it. In trance research it shows up coupled to inward-facing regions, which is unusual.

    Executive control network

    Lateral prefrontal and parietal areas doing deliberate, effortful control. The common hypothesis is reduced ordinary self-monitoring during deep trance — plausible, and not directly demonstrated in ritual possession.

    Autonomic nervous system

    Not a brain network but a body-wide control system: sympathetic (arousal, adrenaline) and parasympathetic (recovery). The most easily measured part of the whole phenomenon, and the part with the best field data.

    The sense of agency: how you normally know you did something

    You do not feel your muscles and deduce authorship. The brain runs something like a prediction check. When you decide to move, a copy of the motor command is used to predict the sensory consequences. If what arrives matches the prediction, the movement gets flagged as mine. The flag is a computation, and like any computation it can produce the wrong answer.

    You already know this from ordinary life. A limb that jerks in half-sleep feels like it happened to you. Automatic writing, ouija boards and ideomotor effects are the classic laboratory demonstrations: people make real movements while sincerely reporting they are not making them. Hypnosis research produces the same dissociation on demand — suggest that an arm is being lifted by something else, and people both lift it and report not lifting it, with brain-imaging differences from the same movement performed voluntarily.

    Supported Agency can be decoupled from action in healthy people. Proposed That ritual possession is this decoupling, given a cultural name.

    The sense of self: how “I” can go quiet

    Selfhood is not one thing. It is at least: a body that feels like yours, a viewpoint located in that body, a continuous story that connects yesterday to today, and a monitor that tracks how you are being seen. Each of these can be manipulated separately in the lab. The rubber-hand illusion shifts body ownership in ninety seconds with two paintbrushes. Vestibular and temporoparietal stimulation can shift the felt location of the viewpoint. Absorption quiets the social monitor.

    So the experience “this is not me, someone else is here” does not require an outside entity to be present. It requires the self-construction system to be running differently — plus a culture that supplies a ready-made name for the person who has arrived.

    Attention: the quiet powerhouse

    Attention decides what gets processed richly and what gets processed barely at all. Narrow it hard enough and things happening in plain sight are missed — the inattentional-blindness experiments are a party trick precisely because the effect is so large. Now apply that inside a ritual: sensations that do not fit the deity's script get less processing, the ones that do fit get amplified, and the whole experience becomes more coherent and more convincing from the inside than it would otherwise be.

    This is also the entry point for pain modulation, because pain is one of the things attention can turn up and down.

    Predictive processing: the brain as a guesser

    The modern working framework in neuroscience says the brain is not passively receiving the world. It is constantly generating predictions about what signals should arrive, and mostly processing the difference between prediction and arrival. Perception is the brain's best current guess, corrected as needed.

    Bodily signals are exactly the noisy kind. Your brain has no precise readout of what your organs are doing; it estimates. Inside a ritual, a chest tightening or a wave of heat is an ambiguous signal meeting an extremely confident prior. The output can be a vivid, unmistakable experience of arrival — with no deception involved anywhere, and no entity required.

    Supported Prior expectations demonstrably shape perception, including bodily perception. Proposed The full predictive-processing account of possession is a theoretical framework, not a tested result.

    05 / chemistry

    Dopamine and friends, without the hype

    Neurotransmitter explanations are the most abused part of this topic. Each of these molecules does many jobs in many circuits; none of them is a switch labelled “trance”. Here is what each one actually does, and how far the ritual connection can honestly be pushed.

    What the Balinese research actually showed

    The claim: physiological changes can be measured during real possession trance, in the field, not in a laboratory imitation.

    The study: a Japanese–Indonesian team built a portable multi-channel EEG telemetry system able to record from people moving vigorously, and used it on three healthy Balinese men performing a ritual dedicatory drama.

    The result: one of the three entered possession during the drama. His EEG showed increased power in the theta and alpha bands during trance. The other two, who did not enter trance, showed no such change. Importantly, the possessed man's EEG showed no pathological findings — no epileptic discharges.

    What it does show: that possession trance is a distinguishable physiological state, not merely a social performance with no bodily correlate, and that it does not look like a seizure.

    What it does not show: anything about cause. One trance subject is a sample of one. Theta and alpha increases also occur in drowsiness, meditation and relaxed attention, so the pattern is not a fingerprint of possession. And the study says nothing whatsoever about whether a deity was present — an EEG cannot address that question even in principle.

    Also worth knowing: claims circulating online that Balinese trance research “proved endorphin and dopamine surges cause possession” overstate the literature badly. Hormonal work on ritual states exists, but it is sparse, mostly correlational, and largely conducted on exertion and dance rather than on possession itself.

    06 / the chain

    Why the blows do not seem to hurt him

    This is the part that convinces onlookers more than anything else. It is also the part where science has the most to say, because pain has been studied intensively for sixty years — and the central finding is that the injury and the hurting are two different things.

    Nociception is not pain

    Nociception

    The detection and transmission of tissue-damaging events. Specialised nerve endings fire, signals travel the spinal cord upward. This is measurable machinery and it happens whether or not anyone is conscious — it occurs under general anaesthesia.

    Pain

    The conscious experience. It is produced by the brain, using nociceptive input as one ingredient among several — alongside attention, expectation, emotional state, meaning and context. This is why the same injury hurts differently on different days.

    The gap between them is not a loophole or a trick. It is the standard modern understanding, built from the gate-control theory onward and confirmed by decades of imaging and clinical work. Soldiers with severe battlefield wounds have long been observed to request less pain relief than civilians with comparable surgical wounds — the injury was similar, the meaning was not.

    The seven dials that change how much it hurts

    Attention

    Pain demands attention, but attention directed elsewhere reliably reduces reported pain and the brain response to it. In deep ritual absorption, attention is committed almost entirely elsewhere.

    Expectation

    Placebo analgesia is a genuine, measurable phenomenon: expecting relief produces relief, and part of that effect is opioid-mediated — blocking opioid receptors with naloxone reduces it. If everyone including the oracle expects that the deity feels no pain, the expectation itself is doing pharmacological work.

    Meaning

    The same stimulus interpreted as harm hurts more than one interpreted as devotion, achievement or sacrifice. Meaning is not a metaphor here; it changes reported intensity and unpleasantness.

    Emotional arousal

    High arousal can produce stress-induced analgesia. It can also amplify pain when the emotion is fear of harm. Direction depends on what the arousal means to the person.

    The social environment

    An audience, shared belief and a valued role all shift pain reports. Field research on ordeal rituals repeatedly finds much lower distress than an outside observer would predict.

    Dissociation and endogenous opioids

    Dissociative states are associated with reduced pain reporting, and the body's own opioid system damps nociceptive signalling under exertion and stress. These are the two most likely direct contributors on the day.

    The line that must not be blurred

    Reduced pain does not mean reduced injury. Skin still splits, muscle still bruises, iron still carries tetanus risk, wounds still get infected. Everything described above changes the experience, not the tissue. A person who feels nothing during the ritual can still need a tetanus booster, wound cleaning and stitches afterwards — and the temporary anaesthesia of trance can delay seeking that care. This is the one place where the science has a practical, non-negotiable implication.

    What the ordeal-ritual research has found

    The most systematic body of work here comes from studies of Thaipusam in Mauritius, where devotees pierce their bodies with skewers and hooks and carry heavy frames for hours. One widely cited study of 86 men compared a low-intensity ritual (singing and collective prayer) with the high-intensity kavadi: both performers and mere observers of the painful ritual donated more to a charity afterwards, and higher reported pain predicted larger donations and a broader sense of shared identity. A later study using wearable monitors on 39 men before, during and after the festival found no lasting harm to sleep, activity or stress markers among those who pierced themselves, and self-reported health and quality of life were, if anything, better.

    Supported People in ordeal rituals tolerate stimuli that outsiders expect to be unbearable, and report modest pain. Proposed The specific mix of mechanisms — how much is endorphins, how much attention, how much expectation — has not been separated out in any field study. Note These studies are of piercing rituals in Mauritius; transferring their conclusions to chain-striking in a Rajasthani village is an assumption, not a finding.

    07 / the faking question

    Why he does not look like he is acting

    “Is he faking?” treats this as a binary. The honest picture is a spectrum, and a single episode can sit at several points on it at once — or move along it as the hour passes.

    Why “he is definitely faking” is not a scientific conclusion

    • You cannot read voluntariness off behaviour. A movement generated with full deliberation and one generated automatically can look identical to an observer — and identical to the person too, if their agency signal has shifted.
    • Fluent, well-formed behaviour is not evidence of deliberation. Deep practice makes complex behaviour automatic; that is what practice is for.
    • Suggestible, highly absorbed individuals demonstrably produce sincere involuntary reports under hypnosis, verified against brain-imaging differences from voluntary imitation.
    • Preparation is not proof of fraud. A practitioner who fasts, bathes and prepares is doing what any skilled performer of a demanding state does.

    Why “a supernatural being definitely entered him” is not one either

    • No measurement in the scene distinguishes “deity present” from “brain and body in an altered state, interpreted through local belief”. Both predict everything observed.
    • Sincerity is not evidence of the content of a belief. People sincerely report experiences whose stated causes turn out to be wrong all the time — that is ordinary human cognition, not stupidity.
    • The cultural specificity is a problem for the supernatural reading: the identity that arrives is always locally available, and always behaves in locally expected ways.

    And yes — deliberate faking also exists

    It would be dishonest to leave this out. Possession is a socially powerful role: it confers authority, income and deference. Cases of conscious imitation have been documented, and the incentives are obvious. The scientific position is not “nobody fakes”. It is that you cannot tell which case you are looking at by watching, and being sincere and being in an altered state are not the same question as being right about the cause.

    08 / culture

    One capacity, many gods

    If altered states were caused by a particular deity, they would be rare and confined to that deity's territory. They are not. They appear everywhere — and the identity that arrives is always one the local culture already knows.

    This is the distinction that resolves a lot of arguments. Biological capacity is the raw ability to enter and sustain a state in which attention narrows, self-monitoring drops and agency is felt differently. It appears to be part of the standard human toolkit, unevenly distributed like musical ability. Cultural interpretation supplies the content: who arrives, what they say, what it means, what happens next.

    So an Indian villager experiences a village devta; a Balinese practitioner is entered by a locally specific spirit during a temple drama; a West African initiate is mounted by an orisha; a Pentecostal believer is filled with the Holy Spirit and speaks in tongues; a Siberian shaman travels and negotiates with spirits. Nobody in the Rajasthan courtyard is ever entered by an orisha. Nobody in Bali receives the Holy Spirit. That pattern is a strong clue: the biology sets the stage, the culture writes the cast list.

    Supported Cultural shaping of altered-state content is one of the most robust findings in psychological anthropology. Comparable work on voice-hearing found that experiences reported in the USA, Ghana and India differed systematically along cultural lines while the underlying phenomenon was shared.

    09 / the loop

    The community is part of the mechanism

    Nothing in this phenomenon happens inside one skull. The audience is not watching the event; the audience is a component of it.

    The forces in the loop

    Social reinforcement

    Behaviours followed by attention, respect and confirmation tend to recur and sharpen. This operates below deliberate intent.

    Authority

    Once a role is recognised, statements made from inside it carry weight they would not carry from the same man on Monday. Authority changes how the audience interprets ambiguity — in the speaker's favour.

    Expectation

    The crowd's expectation is not passive. It shapes which of his behaviours are noticed, remembered and retold, which in turn shapes the next performance.

    Conformity and cultural learning

    Everyone present learns the correct way to behave in the presence of the deity, including how to ask, how to listen, and how to report afterwards. The young learn it by watching.

    Do not collapse this into “peer pressure”

    Peer pressure implies a person going along with something they privately reject. That is not what a feedback loop is. Here, the social input is genuinely changing the state itself: the arousal, the attention, the confidence in the interpretation, even the pain. Calling it peer pressure would be like calling a conversation “sound pressure”. Technically involved, entirely missing the point.

    10 / the queue

    Why people bring their problems here

    A fever that will not break. A marriage that keeps collapsing. A debt. A son who has stopped speaking to the family. Land that two brothers both claim. These are the questions that reach the oracle — and they have something in common.

    Every one of them is a problem where uncertainty is the worst part, where the options are limited, and where the alternatives on offer are expensive, distant or unavailable. In much of rural India the nearest psychiatrist may be hours away, cost a week's earnings, speak a different register of the language, and offer nothing that the family recognises as an explanation. The temple or the courtyard is close, free or cheap, open on a known day, and speaks the family's own moral language.

    Meaning-making

    An explanation — even one an outsider rejects — converts a formless dread into a named thing with a cause and a procedure. Humans tolerate named misfortune far better than senseless misfortune.

    Uncertainty reduction

    Chronic uncertainty is itself corrosive to health and decision-making. A definite answer, even an arbitrary one, ends the paralysis of deliberation.

    Emotional relief and catharsis

    Being heard, publicly, by an authority, about something shameful, is a real intervention. Much of what any therapy does begins with exactly this.

    Social support

    These are family events. Ethnographic work at Indian healing temples has repeatedly emphasised that the whole family participates, stays, and is re-engaged in the sufferer's problem — a level of involvement most clinics cannot supply.

    Behavioural guidance

    The oracle usually prescribes action: go, do, give, wait, apologise, return next month. Action interrupts rumination and restores a sense of control, which is a recognised therapeutic ingredient.

    Hope and expectancy

    Expectation of improvement measurably improves some outcomes — most strongly symptoms mediated by perception and distress, like pain, nausea, fatigue and mood.

    The distinction that must be held

    “This ritual plausibly produces real psychological and social benefits” is a claim about people, and it has substantial support. “The deity is therefore real and is causing the benefits” is a different claim, and none of the evidence above touches it. A ritual can work on a person through entirely ordinary mechanisms while its stated explanation remains untested. Both halves of that sentence matter.

    11 / the grain

    What the handful of grain is doing

    He answers, and presses a few grains into the palm. Ash, thread, water, a knotted cloth — the object varies, the structure does not.

    It makes the encounter portable

    The ritual lasted minutes; the problem lasts months. A physical token carries the authority of that moment home and keeps it available every time it is seen or touched.

    It prescribes behaviour

    Grain has to be kept somewhere, mixed with something, given to someone, returned on a date. Each instruction is a concrete action in a situation that previously offered none.

    It works as a conditioned cue

    An object repeatedly paired with relief and reassurance can come to trigger part of that response on its own. This is ordinary associative learning, demonstrated in both people and animals.

    It carries expectancy

    Received from a deity, in public, after being heard — the object arrives loaded with expectation, which is the active ingredient in placebo responding.

    Placebo, properly understood

    Placebo effects are not imagination and they are not a marker of foolishness. They are real physiological responses produced by the context of a treatment rather than its specific content. Some are opioid-mediated: the classic demonstration is that naloxone, an opioid blocker, reduces placebo analgesia — meaning the expectation had recruited the body's own pain chemistry.

    What placebo effects can do

    • Reduce pain, nausea, fatigue, itching and breathlessness
    • Improve mood, sleep and reported wellbeing
    • Improve self-reported function in some conditions
    • Alter measurable brain and neurochemical responses

    What placebo effects cannot do

    • Clear an infection or kill a parasite
    • Shrink a tumour or correct an obstruction
    • Replace insulin, antibiotics, antiepileptics or antipsychotics
    • Fix a fracture or reverse organ failure

    Susceptibility to placebo effects is not a personality defect or a sign of low intelligence — it is a general property of how brains process expectation, and it operates in doctors, scientists and sceptics alike. The one serious danger is substitution: a remedy that relieves distress while a treatable, dangerous condition goes untreated. That risk is real and has cost lives; it is separate from any judgement about the belief system.

    And once more, the limit: a placebo effect demonstrates that expectation changed something in this person's body. It does not demonstrate that the explanation given for the remedy is correct. Placebo effects occur with sugar pills, saline injections, sham surgery and blessed objects equally.

    12 / the clinic

    Trauma, dissociation, and where medicine actually draws its line

    There is a real research literature linking some possession presentations to trauma and severe stress. It has to be reported honestly, and it has to be kept in its lane.

    The clearest study of this kind was conducted in southwest Uganda: 119 people identified as spirit-possessed by traditional healers were compared with 71 matched non-possessed controls. The possessed group reported significantly more dissociative symptoms — both psychological and bodily — and significantly more potentially traumatising life events, with a significant association between the two. Notably, the participants themselves did not connect their symptoms to those events; the link was visible to the researchers, not to the sufferers.

    Supported In help-seeking, distressed populations, possession presentations are associated with trauma exposure and elevated dissociation. Limitation Everyone in that study was a person with a problem, recruited through healers. You cannot generalise from a clinical sample to a respected village oracle who is not seeking help and has no complaint. That is a basic sampling issue, and it is routinely ignored when this research gets summarised in public.

    Religious trance is not a psychiatric disorder

    Both major systems say so explicitly. ICD-11 requires that a trance or possession trance state be involuntary, unwanted, and not accepted as part of a collective cultural or religious practice, and that it cause significant distress or impairment. DSM-5 similarly excludes states that are a normal part of a broadly accepted collective cultural or religious practice. Under either manual, the Saturday oracle — expected, scheduled, valued, functioning normally the rest of the week — falls outside the diagnosis.

    Where a clinician would start paying attention

    Signals of concern

    • Episodes are unwanted and the person wants them to stop
    • They occur outside the ritual setting — at work, at home, unpredictably
    • They cannot be ended or interrupted
    • Significant distress, or a decline in functioning at work or in the family
    • Injuries that are increasing, untreated, or involve others
    • Large memory gaps outside the ritual, or confusion that persists for days
    • Danger to the person or to anyone else
    • New onset in mid or later life, or with headache, fever, weakness, or first-ever seizures — which need a medical workup, not an interpretation

    Not, by themselves, signals of concern

    • Entering trance at the expected time in the expected place
    • Speaking in a different voice while in that role
    • Remembering little of the episode afterwards
    • Believing sincerely that a deity was present
    • Being respected, consulted or paid for the role
    • Being highly absorbable or highly suggestible

    This page makes no claim about the man in the example. Nobody has examined him, nobody has asked him whether he wants the episodes, and diagnosing a person from a description is malpractice regardless of who is doing it. The purpose of this section is the opposite: to make clear that “possession” and “illness” are separate questions, and that most people in his position are neither ill nor lying.

    13 / India

    Possession and trance in India

    India has one of the world's richest and best-documented landscapes of possession — plural, regionally distinct, and studied by anthropologists and psychiatrists who often disagree with each other productively.

    Village deity and oracle traditions

    Across rural India, local deities are consulted through a human medium who is entered at fixed times. The medium is typically a recognised office-holder rather than a random person; entry is often hereditary or by a recognised calling, and the role carries obligations as well as authority. This is the tradition the opening example belongs to.

    Himalayan oracles

    In Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand and adjoining regions, village gods speak through mediums who deliver judgements on disputes, illness and misfortune. William Sax's long ethnography of ritual healing in the central Himalayas documents how these proceedings function partly as a justice mechanism: grievances too dangerous to voice directly — against a landlord, a husband, a caste superior — can be raised when the deity is the one speaking. That social function is invisible if you only ask “is the god real?”.

    South Indian bhūta traditions

    In coastal Karnataka, bhūta kola involves elaborately costumed performers who become the vehicle of a bhūta (a powerful spirit, often a deified ancestor or local figure), pronounce on disputes and receive homage. Peter Claus's work on Tulu traditions established a point that recurs in Indian scholarship: possession here is deeply embedded in social structure, caste relations and land rights, not merely in individual psychology.

    Healing temples and the Balaji literature

    Mehandipur Balaji in Dausa district, Rajasthan, is the most studied site of its kind in India. Sufferers and their families stay for extended periods; the afflicted often display possession behaviour publicly, and the framework is exorcistic rather than oracular. Two distinct literatures exist. Psychiatric surveys, going back to a 1981 study in the Indian Journal of Psychiatry of patients attending the temple, have examined what conditions bring people there. Anthropological and clinical-anthropological work — Antti Pakaslahti's fieldwork from 1991 onward, Sudhir Kakar's earlier account, Graham Dwyer's ethnography — has focused instead on the process: the family stays involved, the affliction is externalised into a spirit rather than into the sufferer's character, and the treatment has a public, communal structure.

    That second observation matters and is worth stating carefully. Externalising a problem can be therapeutically useful: a family that says “a spirit is troubling her” may treat her better than one that says “she is mad”. Whether the outcome is better than clinical treatment, for which conditions, and at what cost, is a genuinely open empirical question — and the risks are real, including neglect of treatable illness, extended stays, financial strain and, at some sites, coercive practices.

    Indian psychiatric literature

    Dissociative presentations are a recurring topic in Indian psychiatry, partly because they are common in general-hospital and rural settings and frequently framed by families in possession terms. The literature includes cases where possession language accompanies epilepsy, psychosis or depression — which is the clinically important point. Reports of collective episodes in villages and schools also appear regularly in the Indian Journal of Psychiatry and comparable journals, illustrating how quickly a possession interpretation can spread through a group under stress.

    Note on the evidence Much of this literature is ethnographic, case-based or descriptive. There are very few controlled studies, almost no physiological measurement of Indian ritual trance, and no published brain imaging of an Indian oracle in state. The honest summary: India offers extraordinary material and very thin quantitative data.

    15 / the voice

    Why the voice and face change

    This is often the most unsettling detail for an observer. It is also among the more tractable.

    Learned cultural scripts

    The deity has a known manner. Decades of exposure supply a detailed template — register, vocabulary, posture, tempo. Producing it requires no deliberation, in the same way that speaking your mother tongue requires no grammar lookup.

    Altered self-representation

    If the system that normally maintains “I am this person” is running differently, behaviour is no longer being filtered through the usual self-concept. The habitual constraints on how one speaks to elders, or to a landlord, loosen.

    Role enactment without deception

    Role enactment is a technical term, not an accusation. Absorbed people take on roles fully and sincerely; actors describe losing themselves in a part, and possession involves far stronger cultural stakes than any stage role.

    Automatic behaviour

    Highly practised sequences run with little conscious oversight — which is also why they can feel, from the inside, like something else is producing them.

    Arousal changing the voice itself

    This part is straightforwardly physical. High arousal alters breathing, laryngeal tension and muscle tone, which genuinely changes pitch, loudness and timbre. Some of the voice change is mechanics, not identity.

    Social reinforcement over time

    Whichever vocal and gestural features the community recognises most strongly as the deity get reinforced across years, so the portrayal converges on the community's expectation.

    Proposed Each mechanism above is supported in general psychology; their combination in ritual possession is a reasonable reconstruction rather than a measured account. No one has systematically analysed the acoustics of oracle speech against the same speaker's ordinary voice — which would be a genuinely useful and quite cheap study.

    16 / the hits

    “But he knew something he could not have known”

    This is the argument that actually persuades people — far more than the chain or the voice. It deserves a careful answer rather than a dismissal, because the experience behind it is real: people do walk away stunned.

    Before reaching for an extraordinary explanation, the ordinary ones have to be eliminated. They usually are not, because eliminating them requires records nobody keeps.

    Prior knowledge

    He has lived in this village his whole life. He knows the families, the feuds, the debts, who drinks, whose son left for the city, whose daughter's marriage talks collapsed last winter. This is the largest factor and the most consistently underestimated.

    Information leakage

    The petitioner arrives with a family, and someone in that family spoke to someone. The question itself carries information: how it is phrased, what is emphasised, what is carefully avoided.

    Body language and reaction reading

    Humans are good at reading faces and posture, especially familiar faces. A guess can be adjusted mid-sentence on the strength of a flinch or a nod, without either party noticing the correction.

    Questioning technique

    “There is a problem in the house — an older woman?” is a question that looks like a statement. Confirmations from the petitioner get absorbed into what is later remembered as knowledge he supplied.

    High-probability statements

    Some things are simply likely. Most families with a sick elder have a money worry. Most stalled marriages involve a dowry dispute or a caste objection. Naming the likely is not trickery, but it is not knowledge either.

    Vagueness that the listener completes

    “Something from the past is blocking the path” fits almost any life. The listener supplies the specific event — and then remembers the specific event as having been named.

    Confirmation bias and selective memory

    Hits are vivid, retold and become village stories. Misses are forgotten, reinterpreted as tests, or explained as the petitioner's lack of faith. Nobody is keeping score, so the record that survives is systematically filtered.

    Hindsight bias

    After an outcome is known, an ambiguous earlier statement gets remembered as having predicted it. This is one of the best-replicated effects in cognitive psychology and it is invisible from the inside.

    Notice what this list does not say: it does not say he is running a con. Every one of these mechanisms operates without deliberate deception, in people who fully believe what they are saying. A man in an altered state, drawing on a lifetime of village knowledge, reading faces, speaking in fragments, in front of an audience primed to find meaning, will produce apparent hits — and will be as surprised by them as anyone.

    And if a claim survives all of that?

    Then you have something genuinely interesting, and the correct response is not belief and not dismissal. It is controlled testing. That is what the next section is for. A claim that cannot be tested stays untested; a claim that can be tested and is not, remains an anecdote no matter how many people witnessed it.

    17 / the test

    How you would actually test an oracle

    This is a sketch of a fair study — fair in both directions. It is designed so that a genuine ability would show up clearly, and so that ordinary explanations could not produce a false positive.

    What each safeguard is actually for

    Preregistration

    You publish the hypothesis, sample size, scoring rules and analysis before collecting data. This blocks the commonest way false results appear: deciding after the fact which measure counts as success.

    Randomisation

    Targets are selected by a random process, not chosen by a person. Human choice is patterned and guessable; randomisation removes that channel entirely.

    Blinding

    Nobody in the room — not the oracle, not the sitter, not the researcher — knows the target. Otherwise unconscious cueing does the work, and it does not require anyone to cheat.

    Controls

    Run non-oracle participants through the identical procedure. Ordinary people making sensible guesses score above zero; the only meaningful question is whether the oracle scores above them.

    Adequate sample size

    Twenty sessions cannot detect a small effect and will produce eye-catching noise. The number of trials is calculated in advance from the smallest effect worth caring about.

    Objective scoring

    Judges who do not know which target belongs to which reading rank the transcripts. This defeats the vagueness problem: a statement that fits every target cannot help the ranking.

    Two things that make this study worth doing properly

    First, it is respectful. It does not presume fraud; it presumes that a serious claim deserves a serious test, conducted with the practitioner's consent and in a way he could in principle pass.

    Second, it is informative either way. A negative result tells you that the ordinary explanations suffice. A well-replicated positive result would be one of the most important findings in the history of psychology — which is exactly why the bar is set this high. Related claims have been tested this way before; the pattern in the published record is that effects tend to shrink or disappear as blinding tightens and preregistration is enforced, which is itself informative.

    18 / limits

    What science is not equipped to settle

    This section exists so that nothing above gets over-read. The reach of a method is part of its honesty.

    Science can investigate

    • Brain activity, blood flow, electrical rhythms
    • Heart rate, hormones, breathing, skin conductance
    • Behaviour, speech, movement, facial expression
    • Reported pain and measured injury
    • Memory, attention, suggestibility, absorption
    • Social effects on individuals and groups
    • Whether a specific, repeatable claim holds up under blinding

    Science cannot directly establish

    • Whether a deity exists
    • Whether a particular being entered a particular body
    • The ultimate nature of consciousness or spirit
    • Whether a life has meaning, or which meaning
    • What ought to be sacred

    The reason is structural, not political. Scientific investigation works on claims that make some observable difference — if a claim is true you should see X, if false you should see Y. A supernatural agent described as undetectable in principle makes no such difference, so no instrument engages with it. That is a limit on the method, not a disproof of the claim.

    The same discipline cuts the other way, and it is worth saying plainly to both audiences. A believer cannot claim scientific support for possession, because none exists. A sceptic cannot claim science has refuted it, because refutation was never on the table. What science has done is show that every observable feature of the ritual has a candidate natural explanation — which shifts the burden, without closing the question.

    20 / the whole picture

    One diagram for all of it

    Every arrow, explained

    • Culture → religious belief. A shared inherited framework supplies which beings exist, what they want and how they announce themselves.
    • Religious belief → ritual context. Belief specifies the correct occasion: the day, the place, the offerings, the sequence.
    • Ritual context → expectation. Being in that setting activates highly specific predictions in everyone present, including the medium.
    • Ritual context → social input. The same setting assembles the crowd, the drums, the deference and the queue of petitioners.
    • Expectation + social input → brain and body. Both converge on one nervous system. Expectation sets the priors; social input supplies arousal, rhythm and confirmation.
    • Brain and body → attention and arousal. The first measurable shift: focus narrows, sympathetic activation rises.
    • Attention and arousal → absorption. Sustained narrow focus tips into a qualitatively different state; external input is damped.
    • Absorption → altered agency. With self-monitoring reduced, the signal that normally tags actions as self-caused becomes unreliable.
    • Altered agency → altered self-state. Once actions are not felt as one's own, the most available interpretation — supplied by culture — is that someone else is here.
    • Altered self-state → altered pain. The same shift changes how nociceptive signals are weighted, and the deity's identity carries the expectation of not suffering.
    • All of it → behaviour. Voice, posture, gesture and speech now follow the deity's script.
    • Behaviour → community response. Recognition, deference, questions, gratitude.
    • Community response → cultural reinforcement. The episode is retold; the account becomes part of what everyone knows.
    • Cultural reinforcement → culture. The loop closes. Each Saturday makes the next Saturday's belief, expectation and state a little stronger and a little more reliable.
    21 / the answer

    So what is happening to him?

    The question, in full: when someone says devta mere sharir mein aa gaye, and genuinely behaves differently, strikes himself with a chain, appears to tolerate the pain, and answers people's questions — what does science think may be going on?

    Layer 1 — what is directly observable

    A change in posture, facial expression, voice and vocabulary. Rhythmic movement, trembling, high physical exertion. Signs of autonomic arousal: raised heart rate, sweating, altered breathing. Self-inflicted blows with an iron chain, producing real tissue damage, with markedly less pain behaviour than an observer expects. Fluent verbal interaction with petitioners, in a recognisable persona. Afterwards, a return to ordinary behaviour, often with patchy memory of the episode. A community that treats all of this as the deity's presence. Every item on this list can be recorded on video, on a heart-rate monitor, or in a clinical examination. None of it is in dispute.

    Layer 2 — the mechanisms with scientific support

    A lifetime of cultural learning supplies a detailed template of what possession looks like. Ritual cues and expectation prime the state before it begins. Rhythm, exertion and crowd arousal drive attention into deep absorption, at which point external input is damped and self-monitoring recedes. In that state, the brain's construction of agency becomes unreliable — the actions continue, the feeling of authoring them does not — and the culture immediately supplies a name for whoever is authoring them instead. Pain is modulated hard by attention, expectation, meaning, arousal, dissociation and the body's own opioid system, which is why the chain does not produce the response it would in his kitchen. His answers draw on decades of intimate local knowledge, subtle reading of the petitioner, and statements loose enough for listeners to complete — while the community's memory keeps the hits and discards the misses. Social feedback stabilises the whole arrangement, so that next Saturday runs faster and cleaner than the last.

    Every clause in that paragraph rests on published research, though with two honest qualifications: almost all of it is transferred from adjacent populations rather than measured in Indian village oracles, and the relative weight of each mechanism in any individual case is unknown.

    Layer 3 — what remains outside established evidence

    Whether a deity actually enters his body. No study has demonstrated it. No study has refuted it. No available instrument addresses it. Science has shown that everything visible in the courtyard has a plausible natural account — and that is a different claim from having shown that nothing else is present. Anyone who tells you the matter is settled, in either direction, has stopped doing science and started doing advocacy.

    The scientific question is not simply “fake or real?” The scientifically useful question is: what measurable psychological, neurological, physiological and social processes are occurring, and which claims can be experimentally tested?

    One last thing, aimed at whoever you are about to discuss this with. The man in the courtyard is, in the overwhelming majority of such cases, neither a fraud nor a patient. He is a person doing something human beings have done in nearly every society we know of, in a form his particular village taught him, with a nervous system that works the way everyone's does. Understanding the mechanism does not require insulting him — and insulting him was never any part of understanding the mechanism.

    1. Bourguignon E. Religion, Altered States of Consciousness and Social Change. Ohio State University Press, 1973. The classic cross-cultural survey reporting institutionalised altered states in the large majority of 488 societies. Dated methodology; treat the exact figure as indicative.
    2. Suryani LK, Jensen GD. Trance and Possession in Bali. Oxford University Press, 1993. Book-length treatment by a Balinese psychiatrist; its equation of Balinese trance with multiple personality disorder is contested.
    3. Hyman R. Cold reading: how to convince strangers that you know all about them. The Zetetic (later Skeptical Inquirer), 1977. The standard analysis of the techniques described in section 16.
    4. Forer BR. The fallacy of personal validation: a classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 44(1):118–123, 1949. Why general statements feel personally specific.
    5. Ritchie SJ, Wiseman R, French CC. Failing the future: three unsuccessful attempts to replicate Bem's retroactive facilitation of recall effect. PLoS ONE 7(3):e33423, 2012. A worked example of why replication and preregistration decide these questions.

    Where this page says “supported”, it means several independent studies point the same way. Where it says “proposed”, the idea is coherent and partially evidenced but not established — and a good deal of what you will read elsewhere about the neuroscience of possession sits in that second category while being written as if it were the first.

    Written as an educational explainer. It is not medical advice and makes no diagnosis of any individual. If trance episodes are unwanted, uncontrollable, spreading outside their ritual setting, or producing injuries, that is a reason to see a doctor — and wounds from ritual practice need ordinary wound care and tetanus protection regardless of how little they hurt at the time.
